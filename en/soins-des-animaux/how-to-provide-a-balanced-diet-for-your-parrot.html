How to Provide a Balanced Diet for Your Parrot

Published on October 3, 2026

Photo: Magda Ehlers / Pexels

Parrots in the wild eat a wide variety of foods, but a diet of only seeds is too fatty and lacks vitamins in captivity. This guide explains the basics of a balanced diet and what to avoid. Your needs depend on the species, so an avian vet should confirm what suits your bird.

The foundation: pellets

Most avian vets recommend a good quality pellet food as the base of the diet, because each pellet contains the same balance of nutrients. If your bird currently eats mostly seeds, switch gradually over several weeks, mixing in more pellets each time. Check that the bird keeps eating and that its droppings and weight stay normal, and ask your vet for help if it refuses food.

Fresh vegetables every day

Offer a small bowl of chopped vegetables every day: leafy greens, carrot, sweet potato (cooked), peppers and broccoli. Wash everything first, and remove fresh food after a few hours so it does not spoil.

Fruit and seeds as treats

Fruit contains a lot of sugar, so it should be a small portion: a few pieces of apple (without seeds), berries or melon. Seeds and nuts are high in fat, so give them in small amounts as rewards, not as the main meal.

Fresh water

Change the water every day and wash the bowl often. Fresh water should always be available.

Foods that are dangerous for parrots

  • avocado;
  • chocolate, coffee and tea;
  • alcohol;
  • onion and garlic;
  • apple seeds and fruit pits;
  • very salty, sugary or fatty foods.

Also avoid nonstick cookware (PTFE or Teflon) and some scented products near your bird: the fumes can be fatal to birds.

How much to give

Portions vary a lot between a budgie and a large macaw. Ask your avian vet for portion sizes by species and weight. A good routine is a regular morning and evening feeding with the food removed when it is no longer fresh.

When to see the vet

A parrot that is fluffed up, sits at the bottom of the cage, stops eating, has changes in its droppings or breathes with its tail bobbing needs to be seen by an avian vet the same day. A yearly checkup helps catch nutrition problems early.
