How to Use Plastic Bottles to Create an Automated Watering System for Indoor Plants

Published on October 12, 2026

Photo: Ksenia Chernaya / Pexels

A plastic bottle can act as a small reservoir to water one plant during a short absence. It is not a true automatic system: it helps for a few days to two weeks, but it does not replace checking on your plants. Always test it before you leave.

What you need

  • a clean plastic bottle of 0.5 to 1.5 liters with its cap;
  • a thin nail or a large needle, or sturdy scissors;
  • room-temperature water.

Make the dripper

Pierce the cap with two or three small holes, carefully and holding the cap on a stable surface. Fill the bottle with water, screw on the cap, then turn the bottle upside down and push the neck into the soil a few centimeters from the stem. Water seeps out slowly as the soil dries. Use a small bottle for a small pot and two bottles for a large one.

Adjust the flow

Run a test for several days before you leave. If the water runs too fast, the soil stays soaked and roots can rot; if it does not flow, enlarge the holes slightly. Make sure the pot has a drainage hole and that water does not collect in the saucer.

Which plants it suits

This setup works for plants that like slightly moist soil, such as spider plants or pothos. Avoid it for plants that prefer to dry out between waterings (cacti, succulents, snake plant, ZZ plant): they cope very well with an absence without water.

Precautions

  • Fill the bottle with clean water and rinse it before reusing it, to limit mold and bacteria.
  • Keep bottles and their contents away from children and pets.
  • Several common houseplants (pothos, philodendron, ZZ plant, snake plant) are toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, so keep them out of reach.

For an absence of more than two weeks, ask a friend or neighbor to water your plants instead.
